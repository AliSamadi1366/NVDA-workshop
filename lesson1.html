Lesson 1: What Is a Buffer?

Understanding the components and mechanism of a buffer solution

The Problem: Pure Water Has No pH Stability

Add a single drop of 0.1 M HCl to 100 mL of pure water, and the pH plunges from 7 to about 3. Add a drop of 0.1 M NaOH, and it soars to about 11. Water alone cannot resist pH change — it has no reservoir of acid or base to neutralize what you add.

A buffer solves this. It is a solution that resists pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added.

What a Buffer Contains

A classic buffer has two components:

  • A weak acid (HA) — partially dissociated, so it can neutralize added base
  • Its conjugate base (A⁻) — usually supplied as a salt like NaA, so it can neutralize added acid

Common examples:

  • Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) + sodium acetate (CH₃COONa)
  • Ammonia (NH₃) + ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) — here the weak acid is NH₄⁺, the conjugate base is NH₃
  • Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) + bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) — the blood buffer system
  • Dihydrogen phosphate (H₂PO₄⁻) + hydrogen phosphate (HPO₄²⁻)

How It Works: The Seesaw Mechanism

The weak acid and its conjugate base exist in equilibrium:

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻

When you add strong base (OH⁻): The OH⁻ reacts with HA, converting it to A⁻ and water. The HA concentration drops slightly; A⁻ rises slightly. The equilibrium shifts right to replace some HA, but the pH changes very little because the added OH⁻ is consumed.

When you add strong acid (H⁺): The H⁺ reacts with A⁻, converting it to HA. The A⁻ concentration drops slightly; HA rises slightly. The equilibrium shifts left, but again the pH barely moves.

Key insight: The buffer works because both HA and A⁻ are present in significant amounts. Each neutralizes the other's "enemy" (HA neutralizes base, A⁻ neutralizes acid). The ratio [A⁻]/[HA] changes only slightly, so the pH stays nearly constant.

What Makes a Good Buffer?

  • Both components must be present in reasonable concentrations (typically 0.01–1 M each).
  • The pKa of the weak acid should be close to the desired pH (within ±1 pH unit). If pKa is too far from the target pH, one component will be present in negligible amounts.
  • Concentrations should be comparable. A 10:1 ratio still buffers, but capacity is lopsided.
