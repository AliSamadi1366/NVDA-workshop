The Carbon Footprint of Large Language Models:
A Synthesis of Four Foundational Studies

A comprehensive review tracing the evolution of carbon accounting for LLMs from proxy estimates to life-cycle assessment, identifying five leverage points for emissions reduction.

Authors: Workshop Agent (synthesis) · Sources: Strubell et al. (2019), Patterson et al. (2021), Luccioni et al. (2022), Touvron et al. (2023) · Date: November 2026
Abstract Introduction Unified Comparison (Table 1) Methodological Evolution Five Levers for Reduction Critical Analysis Recommendations Limitations Conclusion References
Abstract. As large language models (LLMs) have grown from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, their computational demands—and corresponding carbon emissions—have become a central concern for the machine learning community. We synthesize findings from four seminal papers spanning 2019–2023: Strubell et al. (2019) on energy and policy considerations for NLP; Patterson et al. (2021) on carbon emissions of large neural network training; Luccioni et al. (2022) on the life-cycle carbon footprint of BLOOM; and Touvron et al. (2023) on Llama 2. Together, these works trace a trajectory from early proxy-based warnings to systematic in-production measurement, architectural innovation, life-cycle assessment, and industrial-scale deployment with carbon accounting. We identify five key leverage points for reducing emissions: (1) algorithmic efficiency through sparse activation, (2) hardware specialization via domain-specific accelerators, (3) geographic optimization of datacenter location and carbon-free energy procurement, (4) life-cycle accounting including embodied emissions, and (5) transparency through standardized reporting. Patterson et al. demonstrate that in principle, the combination of model architecture, processor, and datacenter choices can reduce carbon footprints by up to 100–1000× across independent axes; demonstrated reductions for comparable tasks reach ~130× (GShard-600B vs. GPT-3, net CO₂e). Yet adoption remains uneven. We conclude with recommendations for researchers, conferences, cloud providers, and hardware vendors to institutionalize carbon-aware ML practices.

1. Introduction

The past five years have witnessed exponential growth in language model scale: from BERT (340M parameters, 2019) to GPT-3 (175B, 2020) to Llama 2 (70B, 2023) and beyond. This scaling has delivered remarkable capabilities but at escalating computational cost. Training a single large model can consume megawatt-hours of electricity and emit hundreds of tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e)—comparable to the annual emissions of dozens of cars or hundreds of transatlantic flights.

Four papers form the backbone of the community's understanding of this problem:

  1. Strubell, Ganesh & McCallum (2019) — "Energy and Policy Considerations for Deep Learning in NLP" — the first systematic quantification of financial and environmental costs of NLP model training, including a case study of neural architecture search (NAS) revealing massive hidden costs.
  2. Patterson et al. (2021) — "Carbon Emissions and Large Neural Network Training" (Google) — a comprehensive analysis of five large models (T5, Meena, GShard, Switch Transformer, GPT-3) demonstrating that model architecture, processor choice, and datacenter location jointly enable up to 100–1000× carbon reduction in principle, with demonstrated reductions of ~130× for sparse vs. dense models at matched capability.
  3. Luccioni, Viguier & Ligozat (2022) — "Estimating the Carbon Footprint of BLOOM" — the first full life-cycle assessment (LCA) of a 176B-parameter model, accounting for both operational energy and embodied emissions from hardware manufacturing.
  4. Touvron et al. (2023) — "Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models" (Meta) — an industrial-scale deployment (3.3M GPU-hours, 539 tCO₂e gross) with full carbon accounting, market-based offsetting, and open release to amortize training costs across the community.

This synthesis extracts convergent findings, highlights methodological evolution, identifies remaining gaps, and provides a unified comparison framework.

3. Methodological Evolution: From Proxies to Life-Cycle Assessment

3.1 Proxy-Based Estimation (Strubell et al., 2019)

Strubell et al. measured GPU/CPU power draw during training on a single Titan X, extrapolated to cloud hardware using thermal design power (TDP) and average US datacenter PUE (1.58). Energy converted to CO₂e using US EPA eGRID average (0.423 kg CO₂e/kWh).

Key Lesson: Extrapolation from TDP and average grid intensity introduces order-of-magnitude errors. The NAS re-analysis (Patterson Appendix D) shows actual Google-internal NAS emissions were 3.2 tCO₂e vs. Strubell's 284 tCO₂e estimate—a cautionary tale for proxy-based accounting.

Limitations acknowledged by authors: TDP overestimates actual power; US average grid misrepresents cloud provider emissions; no embodied emissions; NAS cost initially overestimated by ~88× due to hardware/datacenter assumptions (later corrected by Patterson et al., Appendix C/D).

3.2 Measured Systems in Production Datacenters (Patterson et al., 2021)

Patterson et al. used actual measurements from Google's production infrastructure: measured system power (memory, fans, CPU host, network), actual datacenter PUE (1.10 Georgia, 1.08 Iowa), and hourly carbon intensity of the local grid. They introduced gross vs. net CO₂e—the latter accounting for 24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE) procurement via long-term contracts.

Key Advance: Measured performance/Watt diverges significantly from theoretical: TPU v3 is 2.0× better than V100 in practice, despite V100 leading on paper (Figure 5). Extrapolation tools (ML Emissions Calculator, Green Algorithms) differ from measured values by 0.53–2.42× (geometric means 0.92 and 1.48).

3.3 Full Life-Cycle Assessment (Luccioni et al., 2022)

Luccioni et al. applied ISO 14040/14044 LCA methodology to BLOOM, covering:

  • Manufacturing: Server, GPU, and infrastructure embodied emissions (Boavizta database v1.0)
  • Operational: Dynamic power during training (117 days wall-clock on 384 A100 GPUs, French grid ~57 gCO₂e/kWh)
  • Deployment: Inference API emissions modeled over 1.5M requests

Result: 24.7 tCO₂e (operational) vs. 50.5 tCO₂e (full LCA)—embodied emissions roughly equal operational on a low-carbon grid. Inference: ~0.003 tCO₂e/day at deployment scale modeled.

3.4 Industrial Scale with Market-Based Offsetting (Touvron et al., 2023)

Meta reports: 3.3M GPU-hours on A100-80GB (350–400W TDP), 539 tCO₂e gross, 100% offset via Meta's sustainability program (conventional offsets + Renewable Energy Certificates). Training data cutoff: September 2022; tuning data through July 2023. Model card includes carbon footprint—setting a transparency standard.

Critical Distinction: Llama 2 uses market-based accounting (RECs, annual matching, any geography). Patterson et al. distinguish this from 24/7 CFE (hourly, local grid matching). The former is financial accounting; the latter is structural decarbonization. Meta's offsets do not imply the training workload ran on carbon-free energy hour-by-hour.

Finding: Sparsely activated mixture-of-experts (MoE) models achieve >10× compute reduction at equivalent or better accuracy (Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2021). Parameter count decouples from compute—a fundamental shift. Patterson et al. note: "significant power might be saved by revisiting accuracy requirements" (Kaplan et al., 2020).

Key insight: The same hardware running the same model emits >20× less in Oklahoma vs. Georgia due to wind enabling 24/7 CFE matching. Google now schedules large training jobs in high-CFE regions.

4.4 Life-Cycle Accounting: Embodied Emissions Matter

Luccioni et al. reveal that for BLOOM (trained on low-carbon French grid), embodied emissions ≈ operational emissions (25.8 vs. 24.7 tCO₂e). On higher-carbon grids, operational dominates; on clean grids, embodied becomes majority.

Implication: As grids decarbonize, hardware manufacturing becomes the limiting factor. Extending hardware lifetime, improving manufacturing efficiency, and circular economy approaches gain importance. No other study in this synthesis quantifies embodied emissions.

4.5 Inference: The Hidden Majority

All four papers focus primarily on training, yet Patterson et al. cite Google-internal data: inference accounts for 60–90% of total ML energy over a model's lifetime (Patterson et al., Section 4.7). Luccioni et al. model BLOOM inference (~0.003 tCO₂e/day for 1.5M requests/day API) but acknowledge usage uncertainty. Strubell and Touvron do not quantify inference.

5. Critical Analysis: Gaps and Tensions

5.1 The Efficiency Paradox (Jevons Paradox)

Patterson et al. note: "the most sustainable energy is the energy you don't use." Yet efficiency gains historically enable larger models, not lower total emissions. GShard-600B uses 55× less energy than GPT-3 per unit capability, but the field responds by building 1,500B-parameter Switch Transformers. Absolute emissions may not decrease unless efficiency is paired with sufficiency constraints or demand-side management.

5.2 Carbon Offsets vs. Structural Reduction

Touvron et al. offset 100% of Llama 2's 539 tCO₂e via Meta's sustainability program (conventional offsets + RECs). Patterson et al. distinguish conventional offsets (annual matching, any geography) from 24/7 CFE (hourly, local grid matching). The latter is structural decarbonization; the former is financial accounting. The community risks conflating them.

Recommendation: Papers should specify which accounting method they use (location-based vs. market-based; gross vs. net; hourly vs. annual). Model cards should include an "accounting_method" field—aligned with emerging standards from MLCommons Carbon Aware Working Group and Partnership on AI.

5.3 Equity of Access

Strubell et al. warned: "limiting this style of research to industry labs hurts the NLP community." Three responses appear in the corpus:

  • Patterson et al.: Cloud democratizes access (Alibaba, AWS, GCP, Azure); custom accelerators available via cloud.
  • Luccioni et al.: BLOOM trained on public French HPC (Jean Zay, GENCI allocation)—a national-infrastructure model.
  • Touvron et al.: Open release amortizes 3.3M GPU-hours across all users; fine-tuning/inference still require GPUs.

The synthesis reveals three distinct equity strategies: cloud credits, public HPC allocations, and open weights. All three are needed; none alone suffices.

5.4 Measurement vs. Extrapolation Gap

Patterson et al. show extrapolation tools differ from measured values by 0.53–2.42×. Extrapolation systematically overestimates because it uses TDP not measured power, and average grid not marginal/locational intensity. Mandatory measurement infrastructure (CodeCarbon/Carbontracker integrated in training loops) is needed.

6. Recommendations

For Researchers

  1. Measure, don't estimate: Integrate CodeCarbon or Carbontracker in training scripts; log actual GPU power, hours, datacenter location.
  2. Report full stack: Hardware, PUE, grid intensity (gross + net if CFE applies), embodied emissions (even if approximate via Boavizta).
  3. Prioritize sparse architectures for large-scale work; evaluate dense baselines at matched compute, not matched parameters.
  4. Publish failed experiments' costs—development overhead (Patterson §4.2) often equals or exceeds final training.
  5. Quantify inference: Model expected usage patterns; report inference energy per 1K tokens.

For Conferences & Journals

  1. Require carbon impact statements for computationally intensive papers (as EACL/NAACL 2021 "Efficient and Green" tracks pioneered).
  2. Accept "negative results" on efficiency—showing a method doesn't improve perf/Watt is valuable.
  3. Standardize reporting format: Structured table (Table 1 of this paper) as supplementary material requirement.

For Cloud Providers

  1. Expose hourly, locational carbon intensity via API (Google Cloud does; AWS/Azure should follow).
  2. Offer "green training" scheduling: automatically queue jobs in high-CFE regions with user consent.
  3. Publish PUE and CFE% per region per month—enable accurate Scope 2 accounting.

For Hardware Vendors

  1. Report measured performance/Watt on standard DNN workloads, not peak TOPS/Watt.
  2. Disclose embodied emissions per device (cradle-to-gate) to enable LCA.
  3. Design for longevity: modular upgrades, repair-friendly architectures.

7. Limitations of This Synthesis

  1. Source selection bias: Four papers only; excludes e.g., Wu et al. (2022) on sustainable AI, Henderson et al. (2020) on emissions tracking, or more recent models (GPT-4, PaLM-2, Nemotron).
  2. Training-focused: Inference synthesis limited by source gaps (Section 4.5).
  3. No primary measurement: All figures derived from reported values; no independent verification.
  4. Temporal snapshot: Methods and grid intensities evolve rapidly (e.g., TPU v4, Oklahoma CFE%, newer GPU generations).
  5. Geographic concentration: Three of four studies reflect US/EU corporate or national infrastructure; Global South perspectives absent.
  6. Language bias: All four papers focus on English-centric models (BLOOM is multilingual but trained on French grid). Carbon intensity of training non-English models in other grids unexplored.

8. Conclusion

The four papers reviewed trace a remarkable arc: from Strubell et al.'s warning that NAS emitted ~284 tCO₂e (proxy) for a 0.1 BLEU gain, to Patterson et al.'s demonstration that the same task can be done ~130× more cleanly via sparse models + TPUs + Iowa wind, to Luccioni et al.'s rigor in showing that even on clean grids, hardware manufacturing doubles the footprint, to Touvron et al.'s industrial-scale deployment with full accounting and open release.

The technical levers are clear and potent: sparse architectures (10–130×), specialized hardware (2–6×), geographic/CFE optimization (6–20×), life-cycle awareness (2× on clean grids), and measurement-based reporting (eliminating 2× extrapolation error). Combined, these can approach the 100–1000× theoretical range Patterson et al. identify across independent axes.

The remaining barriers are incentive structures (publish-or-perish favors scale over efficiency), measurement infrastructure (tools still rely on proxies), governance (no mandatory reporting standard), and accounting ambiguity (offsets vs. structural reduction). The community has the knowledge; it now needs the will to institutionalize carbon-aware ML as default practice, not optional afterthought.
