Lesson 2: The Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

Calculating buffer pH from the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid

← Lesson 1: What Is a Buffer? Lesson 3: Buffer Capacity →

Deriving the Equation

Start with the acid dissociation equilibrium for a weak acid HA:

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻

The acid dissociation constant is:

Kₐ = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

Solve for [H⁺]:

[H⁺] = Kₐ × [HA] / [A⁻]

Take the negative logarithm (base 10) of both sides:

–log[H⁺] = –log Kₐ – log([HA] / [A⁻])

By definition, pH = –log[H⁺] and pKₐ = –log Kₐ. Also, –log([HA]/[A⁻]) = log([A⁻]/[HA]). This gives the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

pH = pKₐ + log₁₀([A⁻] / [HA])

Where [A⁻] is the concentration of the conjugate base and [HA] is the concentration of the weak acid.

What the Equation Tells Us

  • When [A⁻] = [HA], the log term is zero and pH = pKₐ. This is the point of maximum buffering symmetry.
  • For every 10-fold increase in [A⁻]/[HA], pH increases by 1 unit. The log scale means ratio changes have predictable effects.
  • The equation uses concentrations at equilibrium. In practice, we approximate using the initial (analytical) concentrations because the dissociation of HA and hydrolysis of A⁻ are small when both are present.
Practical note: The approximation works well when both [HA] and [A⁻] are ≥ 0.01 M and the pH is within about ±1 of the pKₐ. Outside this range, the full equilibrium calculation (ICE table) is more accurate.

Worked Example

Example: Acetic Acid/Acetate Buffer

Calculate the pH of a buffer containing 0.20 M CH₃COOH and 0.15 M CH₃COONa. The pKₐ of acetic acid is 4.76.

Solution:

pH = pKₐ + log₁₀([CH₃COO⁻] / [CH₃COOH])
pH = 4.76 + log₁₀(0.15 / 0.20)
pH = 4.76 + log₁₀(0.75)
pH = 4.76 + (–0.125)
pH = 4.64

The pH is slightly below the pKₐ because there is more acid than conjugate base.

Designing a Buffer: Choosing the Ratio

If you need a buffer at a specific target pH, rearrange the equation to find the required ratio:

[A⁻] / [HA] = 10^(pH – pKₐ)

Example: You want a phosphate buffer at pH 7.2. The relevant pKₐ is 7.21 (for H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻).

[HPO₄²⁻] / [H₂PO₄⁻] = 10^(7.20 – 7.21) = 10^(–0.01) ≈ 0.98

A nearly 1:1 ratio of HPO₄²⁻ to H₂PO₄⁻ gives pH 7.20.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using pKₐ of the wrong species. For an NH₃/NH₄⁺ buffer, the weak acid is NH₄⁺ (pKₐ = 9.25), not NH₃.
  • Forgetting that dilution changes concentrations but not the ratio. Diluting a buffer 10-fold changes [HA] and [A⁻] equally, so pH stays the same (as long as the approximation holds).
  • Applying the equation outside its valid range. Very dilute buffers or extreme ratios need the full equilibrium treatment.

A buffer contains 0.10 M HA and 0.10 M A⁻. Its pH equals the pKₐ. If you dilute this buffer tenfold with water, what happens to the pH, and why?
