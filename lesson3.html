Lesson 3: Buffer Capacity and Limits

How much acid or base can a buffer absorb before the pH changes significantly?

What Is Buffer Capacity?

Buffer capacity (β) is a measure of how much strong acid or base a buffer can absorb per unit volume before the pH changes by a defined amount (typically 1 pH unit).

Formally: β = dn / (V × dpH), where dn is the moles of strong acid or base added, V is the volume, and dpH is the resulting pH change. In simpler terms:

Buffer capacity ≈ (moles of strong acid or base added) / (volume × pH change)

A buffer with high capacity resists pH change better — you can add more acid or base before the pH shifts noticeably.

Two Factors That Determine Capacity

1. Total Concentration of Buffer Components

The more HA and A⁻ you have, the more acid or base you can neutralize. Doubling both concentrations roughly doubles the buffer capacity.

Example: A buffer with 0.50 M HA + 0.50 M A⁻ has about twice the capacity of 0.25 M HA + 0.25 M A⁻ (at the same ratio).

2. The Ratio [A⁻]/[HA] (How Close pH Is to pKₐ)

Capacity is maximum when [A⁻] = [HA] (pH = pKₐ). As the ratio moves away from 1:1, capacity drops — one component becomes depleted faster.

  • At [A⁻]/[HA] = 1:1, capacity is 100% (by definition)
  • At 10:1 or 1:10, capacity falls to ~40% of maximum
  • At 100:1 or 1:100, capacity is only ~8% of maximum
Rule of thumb: A buffer is most effective within ±1 pH unit of its pKₐ. Outside this range, one component is too dilute to neutralize much added acid or base.

Worked Example: Comparing Two Buffers

Example: Which Buffer Has Greater Capacity?

Buffer A: 0.10 M CH₃COOH + 0.10 M CH₃COONa (total = 0.20 M, ratio = 1:1)

Buffer B: 0.01 M CH₃COOH + 0.01 M CH₃COONa (total = 0.02 M, ratio = 1:1)

Analysis: Both have the same ratio (pH = pKₐ = 4.76), but Buffer A has 10× the total concentration. Buffer A can absorb roughly 10× more acid or base before the pH shifts by 1 unit.

Example: Same Concentration, Different Ratios

Buffer C: 0.10 M HA + 0.10 M A⁻ (ratio = 1:1, pH = pKₐ)

Buffer D: 0.18 M HA + 0.02 M A⁻ (ratio = 1:9, pH ≈ pKₐ – 0.95)

Analysis: Both have the same total concentration (0.20 M). Buffer C has maximum capacity. Buffer D, with a 9:1 imbalance, has only ~40% of Buffer C's capacity against added base (it runs out of A⁻ quickly), though it still has reasonable capacity against added acid (plenty of HA available).

Practical Limits: When a Buffer Fails

  • Component depletion: If you add enough acid to consume all A⁻, or enough base to consume all HA, the buffer is destroyed — it becomes just a weak acid or weak base solution.
  • Dilution: Diluting a buffer reduces capacity proportionally (less total moles per liter), but pH stays constant if the ratio is unchanged.
  • Ionic strength effects: At very high concentrations (> 0.5 M), activity coefficients deviate from 1, and the simple Henderson–Hasselbalch equation becomes less accurate.
  • Temperature: pKₐ changes with temperature, so a buffer's pH shifts if temperature changes.

Choosing a Buffer for an Experiment

When designing a buffer for a real application:

  1. Pick a weak acid with pKₐ within ±1 of your target pH.
  2. Choose a total concentration that gives enough capacity for the expected acid/base load (typically 0.05–0.2 M for lab work).
  3. Check compatibility: The buffer shouldn't react with your sample (e.g., phosphate precipitates Ca²⁺; Tris reacts with some aldehydes).
  4. Consider temperature: Some buffers (like Tris) have large ΔpKₐ/ΔT; others (like phosphate) are more stable.
